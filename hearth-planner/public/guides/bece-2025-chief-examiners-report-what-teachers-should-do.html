BECE 2025 Chief Examiners' Report: What JHS Teachers Should Change

After every BECE, the West African Examinations Council publishes a report from each subject's Chief Examiner. It lists what candidates did well, what they got wrong, and what teachers should do about it. The 2025 report for school candidates is useful because it is specific. This guide summarises its main findings in plain language so you can adjust your teaching and your practice questions. It is our summary, not the report itself, so read the original for the full detail.

The big picture

The Chief Examiners said the papers were within the syllabus and compared well with the previous year. Performance was average in most subjects, poor in Computing, and lower than the year before in Mathematics. Across subjects, the same habits cost candidates marks: not following the paper's instructions, weak spelling and handwriting, copying from the passage or the question, and skipping the working.

Mathematics: working and word problems

Candidates did well on intersection of sets, listing subsets, making a variable the subject, percentages, vectors and probability. The weak areas were simplifying algebraic expressions (named as the most serious), solving equations, turning a word problem into an equation, standard form, converting decimals, and distance-time graphs. Two habits kept costing marks: writing the old cedi sign instead of GH¢, and jumping to an answer with no working shown.

  • Practise simplification every week, not only in the algebra unit.
  • Give word problems and ask learners to write the equation before solving it.
  • Mark working, not only answers, and tell learners why.
  • Practise drawing graphs with a stated scale; many candidates skipped that question altogether.

Science: explain and apply

Candidates recalled facts and handled acids and bases well. They struggled to explain processes, to apply a concept to a new situation, with the practical parts of questions, and with calculations. Wrong spelling of scientific terms ran through many scripts, and many candidates did not finish the four required questions in the time.

  • Ask "explain why" and "what would happen if" regularly, and have learners answer in full sentences.
  • Run the practical work the curriculum asks for, and ask about apparatus, method, observation and precautions.
  • Keep a spelling list of key terms for each unit.
  • Teach time sharing: roughly equal time for each question, easier ones first.

English: full essays and careful language

Many candidates knew the features of formal and informal letters and wrote concise comprehension answers. Many did not reach the full essay length, struggled with paragraphing, grammar, punctuation and spelling, and misspelt literary devices. Some copied parts of the reading passage as answers. Examiners also asked teachers to use literature texts for comprehension and grammar work.

  • Practise a full-length essay in timed conditions.
  • Teach paragraph structure: one idea, support, link.
  • Insist on answers in the learner's own words.
  • Make a short list of literary devices and practise spelling them.

Social Studies, RME and the practical subjects

In Social Studies, candidates explained the term constitution and the causes of the 1948 riots, but struggled with the contributions of the 1992 Constitution, why Ghana became a republic, why society should be educated on human rights, and why people should join a social security scheme. In RME, the weak points included the importance of sacrifice, differences between Idul Fitr and Idul Adha, causes of bribery and corruption, and answering context questions.

In Computing, candidates were weak on flowcharts, file compression, number conversion, data communication models and practical steps such as inserting a hyperlink. In Creative Arts, the problems were drawing an accurate six-part colour wheel, shading, clay preparation and note values. In Career Technology, candidates struggled to describe techniques such as shaping biscuits and to identify a Warrington hammer.

Exam technique to teach

Several Chief Examiners said the same thing: teach the rubric. Learners should know how many questions to answer, which ones are compulsory, how to number and order answers, and how to share their time. Past questions and the Chief Examiners' reports are good practice material for the class, used together.

Using this in your lesson plans and practice

Pick the two or three weakest areas for your subject and give them extra time in your scheme of work. Write practice questions in the style of the real paper: a figure or short context first, then parts that move from recall, to explanation, to application. In short

The 2025 report points to a few fixable habits: show working, explain in full sentences, apply ideas to new situations, spell key terms, finish the paper, and follow the rubric. Build these into weekly practice, not only the weeks before the exam.
