Helping a Child Who Struggles With Mathematics

Many children, and many adults, say they are "not good at maths." Often the real problem is a gap earlier on that was never filled, or a loss of confidence. The good news is that most children can improve with patient help. Here are some ways to support yours.

Find where the gap starts

Maths builds step by step. If your child struggles with fractions, check whether they are sure about multiplication tables and sharing. Ask them to show you a few simple questions and watch where they hesitate. That is the place to start, even if it is below their class level.

Make it real

  • Count and share objects such as seeds, bottle tops or oranges.
  • Let your child handle money at the market and check the change.
  • Measure ingredients when cooking and lengths of cloth or wood.
  • Play number games, such as dominoes, cards or "guess my number."

Practise facts a little at a time

Quick recall of addition and multiplication facts makes later topics easier. Five minutes a day with cards or a chant works better than a long weekly session.

Ask your child to explain

Say "Show me how you did that." Explaining helps your child find their own mistakes. Wrong answers are useful information, not failures. Respond with "Let's see where it changed" instead of "That is wrong."

Do not say "I was bad at maths too"

This can make a child feel that struggling is normal or permanent. Try "This is tricky, and we can work it out." Show that effort leads to progress.

Keep sessions short and positive

Fifteen to twenty minutes is enough. Finish on a question your child can answer, so that they end feeling capable.

Work with the teacher

Ask which topics are causing trouble and which exercises would help. Some schools offer catch-up groups. A teacher can also check whether your child needs help with vision, hearing or other learning needs.

Celebrate progress

Keep a list of things your child has mastered. Looking back at it shows that they are improving, which builds the confidence that maths needs.

In short

Find the earliest gap, practise in short and real-life ways, ask your child to explain, and keep your own attitude positive.
