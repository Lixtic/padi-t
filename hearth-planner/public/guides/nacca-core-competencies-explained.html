The Core Competencies in the Ghanaian Curriculum, Explained Simply

If you have looked at a recent lesson plan template, you have seen a line for core competencies. These are the skills the national curriculum wants learners to build in every subject, not only the facts of that subject. This guide describes them in plain language and shows how to bring them into an ordinary lesson.

Why competencies matter

A learner who can recite a definition but cannot use it has not really learned it. The competencies are about using knowledge: thinking, working with others, communicating and growing as a person. They are meant to run through every lesson, from mathematics to creative arts.

The competencies and what they look like in class

  • Critical thinking and problem solving: learners work out how to solve something rather than being told. Example: ask how many ways they can share 24 oranges fairly.
  • Creativity and innovation: learners make or design something new. Example: invent a game that practises today's spelling words.
  • Communication and collaboration: learners explain ideas and work in groups. Example: pairs take turns to explain a method to each other.
  • Cultural identity and global citizenship: learners connect lessons to Ghanaian culture and the wider world. Example: use local proverbs, foods or festivals in a story or a measurement task.
  • Personal development and leadership: learners take responsibility and reflect on their own learning. Example: group leaders keep time and report back.
  • Digital literacy: learners use technology safely where it is available. Example: look up a picture with the teacher, or practise typing a short sentence.

You do not need all of them in every lesson

Choose the one or two that fit the activity you have already planned. Trying to tick all of them leads to forced, shallow tasks. A group discussion naturally builds communication and collaboration. A "find a better way" problem builds critical thinking.

How to write them in your plan

Name the competency and add a few words saying how it appears. "Communication and collaboration: learners explain their sorting rule to a partner" is better than the bare label, because it shows that you have thought about it.

Noticing them in learners

Watch for small signs: who asks a good question, who helps a classmate, who tries a second method after the first fails. Note these on a class list. They help when you write reports and talk to parents, and they remind you that marks are not the only measure of progress.

In short

Competencies are habits of thinking and working. Pick one or two for each lesson, build them into the activity, and say in your plan how learners will show them.
