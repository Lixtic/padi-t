Starter, Main Instruction and Closure: Running the Three Phases of a Lesson

Most lesson plans in Ghana divide a lesson into a starter, a main instruction phase and a closure. Used well, this structure keeps lessons focused and gives learners time to practise. Here is how to use each phase and roughly how long to give it.

Phase 1: the starter (about 10 minutes)

The starter wakes up the class and links the lesson to what learners already know. It is not a time to introduce new content. Keep it light and quick.

  • Ask two or three questions about last lesson.
  • Show a real object or picture and ask what learners notice.
  • Play a short recall game, such as "stand up if your answer is..."
  • Tell a one-minute story that leads into the topic.

Phase 2: main instruction (about 25 to 40 minutes)

This is where the new learning happens, and it is where many plans are too teacher-heavy. A useful pattern is: explain one idea briefly, show a worked example, try one together, and then let learners practise.

  • Teach in short chunks. After each chunk, learners do something: answer, sort, draw, write.
  • Use pair and group work so that more learners speak. Groups of four to six work in a large class if you give each member a role.
  • Walk around while learners work. This is your best chance to catch mistakes early.
  • Prepare a harder extension for fast finishers and a simpler version for learners who struggle.

Phase 3: closure (about 5 to 10 minutes)

Closure is often skipped when time runs out, but it is where you find out what learners took away. Protect it.

  • Ask learners to say what they learned in one sentence.
  • Give an exit question and collect or glance at answers.
  • Link back to the objective: "Today we wanted to... can we now do it?"
  • Set a short follow-up task if you give homework, and make sure learners can do it alone.

Adjusting for double periods and short lessons

In a 30-minute lesson, shorten the starter to about five minutes and focus the main part on a single idea. In a double period, add a second practice cycle rather than a second new topic.

Watching the clock without rushing

Write approximate times beside each phase in your plan. They are a guide, not a rule. If learners are clearly stuck, it is better to stay with the idea and trim the closure than to move on with half the class lost.

In short

A short, lively starter, a main phase where learners do most of the work, and a protected closure make a lesson that learners can follow and you can assess.
