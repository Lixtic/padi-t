Assessment as, for and of Learning: What Each Means in Your Classroom

Assessment is more than a test at the end of term. The Ghanaian curriculum talks about assessment as learning, assessment for learning and assessment of learning. They sound alike, but each has a different job. This guide explains them with examples you can use tomorrow.

Assessment for learning: help the teacher teach better

This is checking during the lesson so that you can adjust. If many learners are lost, you reteach. If most have it, you move on. Nothing is recorded as a final mark.

  • Ask a question and have everyone answer on a slate or in the air with fingers.
  • Walk around and look at work in progress.
  • Use a thumbs up, sideways or down to show confidence.
  • Give a three-question mini quiz at the start of the next lesson.

Assessment as learning: help learners manage their own learning

Here learners look at their own work and think about it. They learn what good work looks like and what to do next.

  • Give learners a short checklist for a piece of writing and let them tick it.
  • Ask them to write one thing they did well and one thing to improve.
  • Pair learners to check each other's work against an example.

Assessment of learning: sum up what has been learned

This is the class test, end-of-term exam or school-based assessment task that produces a mark or grade. It tells learners, parents and the school where each child stands. Keep it fair by testing what you taught, and in the form you taught it.

A balance for a normal week

Most of your assessing should be for and as learning, because these change what happens next. A few well-designed tasks of learning are enough for the record. If your week is mostly marking, you may be spending effort where it helps least.

Feedback that works

  • Say what is right before what is wrong.
  • Point to one or two things to fix, not twenty.
  • Give learners a chance to correct the work, because correction is where learning happens.
  • Keep a short note of common errors and use it to plan the next starter.

Fairness and recording

Use a marking scheme for written tasks so that marks do not depend on mood. Record marks soon after marking and keep copies of key tasks. These records protect you if a parent or supervisor asks how a grade was reached.

In short

Check during lessons so you can adjust, let learners reflect on their own work, and keep a small number of fair, well-marked tasks for the record.
