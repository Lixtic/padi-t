Better Questions: Moving Learners From Recall to Thinking

The questions you ask decide what kind of thinking learners do. If every question is "What is the capital of...?", learners practise remembering. If you also ask "Why?" and "What if?", they practise reasoning. Many curriculum documents use the idea of depth of knowledge to describe these levels. Here is a simple version you can use without a chart.

Four levels of questions

  • Recall: state a fact or follow a known step. "What is 7 times 8?" "Name the three states of matter."
  • Skill or concept: use a method or explain an idea. "Show how you found the area of this rectangle." "Sort these objects into solids and liquids and say why."
  • Strategic thinking: plan, justify and choose a method. "Which is the cheaper way to buy 12 pencils, and how do you know?"
  • Extended thinking: investigate over time, connect ideas, create. "Design a small school garden. How much space, which crops, and what will it cost?"

You need all four, in the right proportions

Recall questions are useful for quick checks and for building fluency. The risk is spending the whole lesson there. Aim for most of your main-phase questions to be at the second or third level, with a recall warm-up at the start and an occasional project that stretches further.

Turn a recall question into a thinking question

  • Recall: "What is a noun?" Thinking: "Which of these words are nouns, and which are not? Explain one you were unsure about."
  • Recall: "What is the rainy season?" Thinking: "How does the rainy season change what farmers in our area do?"
  • Recall: "Add 25 and 17." Thinking: "Make up a story problem that fits 25 + 17 and solve it two different ways."

Give thinking time

Count silently to five before you accept an answer. Ask learners to talk to a partner first, then call on a pair. This helps shy learners and raises the quality of answers. Avoid always taking the first hand up.

Follow up on answers

A wrong answer followed by a good follow-up question is often more useful than a right answer that is accepted and passed over.

  • "How did you work that out?"
  • "Can someone say it a different way?"
  • "What would happen if we changed this number?"
  • "Is there another answer? How do you know yours is right?"

Plan three or four key questions in advance

It is hard to invent good questions on the spot while managing a class. Write them in your plan, with at least one at the third level. If a question gets blank looks, simplify it or break it into two smaller ones.

In short

Mix quick recall with questions that ask learners to explain, choose and justify. Plan a few of the harder ones in advance and give learners time to think.
