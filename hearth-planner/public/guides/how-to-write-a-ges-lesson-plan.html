How to Write a GES Lesson Plan, Step by Step

A lesson plan is your own map for one lesson. It tells you what learners should be able to do by the end, how you will get them there, and how you will know it worked. Ghana Education Service (GES) schools usually expect a written plan, and head teachers and circuit supervisors often ask to see it. Here is a simple way to write one without spending your whole evening on it.

Start with the details at the top

Write the date, class, subject, number of learners, the strand and sub-strand from your scheme of learning, and the length of the lesson. This looks like paperwork, but it helps you and anyone who reads the plan later to see exactly which part of the curriculum you were covering.

Write the content standard and the learning indicator

The content standard is the broad statement of what learners should know or do, and it comes straight from the curriculum. The indicator is the small, testable piece of it that you will teach today. Copy the standard as written, then write the indicator in your own words so it is clear what success looks like in this one lesson.

  • Keep to one indicator for a short lesson, or two at most for a double period.
  • Check the indicator against the scheme of learning so you do not skip ahead or repeat last week.

Choose a clear objective

Begin with the words "By the end of the lesson, learners will be able to" and then use a verb you can observe, such as list, sort, measure, explain, draw or compare. Avoid verbs like "understand" or "know" on their own, because you cannot see them. If you cannot tell whether a learner has reached the objective, rewrite it.

List your resources before you plan the activities

Write down what you will actually use: the textbook page, a chart, real objects from the community, bottle tops for counting, a story on the board. Resources that cost nothing are fine. Planning them first stops you from writing an activity you cannot carry out on the day.

Plan the three phases

Most lessons run in three parts. In the starter you get attention and connect to what learners already know, usually in about ten minutes. In the main part you teach and learners practise. In the closure you check what was learned and tie it back to the objective.

  • Starter: a quick question, a song, a picture or a recap game linked to the topic.
  • Main instruction: explain one idea, show an example, then let learners try it in pairs or small groups before they work alone.
  • Closure: ask two or three learners to say what they learned, and give a short task as an exit check.

Say what learners will be doing, not only what you will do

A weak plan lists only what the teacher says. A good plan has a column for learner activities too. If you can picture learners talking, sorting, drawing or solving, the lesson will be livelier and you will see problems sooner.

Decide how you will check understanding

Write one or two questions you will ask, or a small task you will mark. Include one question that needs thinking, not only recall. This is your evidence for the next lesson, so keep it short and honest.

Leave room for reflection

After the lesson, write two lines: what worked, and what you will change. This habit is the quickest way to improve, and it makes your next plan easier because you are not starting from nothing.

In short

A good plan is short enough to use and clear enough that another teacher could pick it up and teach from it. Start with the indicator, plan the learner activities, and check your plan against the scheme of learning.
